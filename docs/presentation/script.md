# Kiln Watch — 2-minute pitch script

**[0:00 — Hook]** Every winter, Dhaka's air turns brown. Two culprits get blamed: thousands of brick kilns, and farmers burning crop stubble. NASA satellites have watched those fires for 20 years. But if you plot that record, it lies to you.

**[0:15 — The problem: money shot 1, `money_jump.png`]** In 2012, NASA's new VIIRS sensor arrived. It sees fires four times smaller than MODIS could. Splice the two records and fire in Bangladesh appears to triple overnight. It didn't. That jump is the sensor, not the land.

**[0:35 — Harmonization]** Kiln Watch puts every satellite on one honest scale. On days when Aqua-MODIS and VIIRS both saw the same clear ground, we learn how many MODIS detections one VIIRS detection is worth, by region, month, day or night, with uncertainty from resampling whole seasons. Result: the 2012 jump shrinks to **3%** of its raw size. A statistical break test that screams in the raw record (p = 0.00006) goes quiet after harmonization (p = 0.29). And the Aqua satellite, which flew straight through 2012, lands inside our band.

**[1:00 — The surprise: money shot 2, Story page negative-result card]** We wanted to separate kiln heat from crop fires. So before touching the data, we pre-registered a test: are 3,600 mapped kiln clusters hotter, in satellite eyes, than matched control sites? Answer: **no**. Kilns showed about half the detection rate of controls. Bangladesh's brick kilns are essentially invisible to NASA's fire products. We publish that, not a pretty map built on a false assumption. It matters: anyone using FIRMS fire counts as a kiln proxy is measuring crop fires.

**[1:30 — Product]** What ships: a calendar for every one of Bangladesh's 64 districts and 500+ upazilas. Every day since 2003 on one scale, the normal range, unusual days, critical periods, the current season updated daily, and Aman and Boro harvest-window splits. Draw any box on the map. Download everything. It works offline and in Bangla.

**[1:50 — Close]** Kiln Watch: twenty years of fire, one honest scale, and the courage to report what the satellites can't see.
